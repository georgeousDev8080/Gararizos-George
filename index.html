06

TEACHING PHILOSOPHY

01

SIMPLIFY COMPLEXITY

Breaking complex concepts into digestible, progressive modules students can build on immediately.

e.g. Before touching a single line of coroutine code, I draw the execution flow on a whiteboard — three boxes, two arrows. Only then do we open the IDE.
02

HANDS-ON LEARNING

Every session ships something. Students leave with a working system they built themselves, not just notes.

e.g. Even a first-session student builds a bouncing ball that reacts to a key press — small enough to finish, real enough to feel the feedback loop of game development.
03

FOSTER CREATIVITY

Open briefs over closed assignments — students discover their own creative voice through constraints, not despite them.

e.g. "Make something that feels scary — you have 30 minutes and no assets." We then discuss how their mechanic choices created tension far better than any jumpscare would.
04

REAL-WORLD APPLICATION

Theory only sticks when students see where it lives in a shipped product.

e.g. When teaching event systems, I pull up a real Unity Asset Store package, trace its event bus live, and show exactly why the original dev made that call — textbook examples rarely survive contact with a real codebase.
05

CONTINUOUS MENTORSHIP

The session is the starting point, not the finish line. Growth happens in the weeks that follow.

e.g. I follow up with workshop attendees 3–4 weeks later with a single question: "What did you ship?" That question alone keeps momentum alive better than any homework assignment.
06

STAY CURRENT

Students deserve to learn techniques that work today, not last cycle's best practices.

e.g. I rebuild my core demo projects on every Unity LTS release — if a pattern has a cleaner equivalent in the new API, my students learn the new one. I also attend events like JOIST specifically to hear where practitioners say the industry is heading next.
